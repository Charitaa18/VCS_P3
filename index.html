Project 3: Usability and Interface

By Charitaa Bagry

When I first thought about usability and interface, I thought it was mostly about how something looks on a screen. With all the readings we've done so far, I see it's a topic far bigger than the box I had put it in. When going through the class's microresponses, I realized they see it as something bigger too. Initally the idea that kept coming up was that if people keep struggling with something, the problem is the design and not the person.

The Norman door video was a starting point for many of us. Andrea chose Don Norman's line that an ideal door is one where you are "not even aware that I had opened the door." Jasmine focused on discoverability, that you should be able to "discover what operations I can do." Jiho brought this into everyday life with stove knobs lined up in a row even though the burners sit in a 2x2 grid. Fan Lin added Apple's idea that "simplicity isn't minimalism," and Gia connected this to Jonathan's point that small details matter, like the iPhone lock screen now giving quick access to the flashlight and camera.

When Matias shared a LinkedIn post comparing UX and UI to potatoes and mashed potatoes, Andrea took it to mean the two can't be separated, since how something looks affects how people use it. Felix disagreed at first, seeing UX as something broader, but came to like the analogy because it showed UI is a big part of UX, not all of it.

On familiarity, Juliette pointed out that people admire a creative book, but get frustrated online when buttons or hamburger menus don't work as expected. Jasmine, coming from a background in graphic design, realized creativity that works in print can backfire on screen. Jon described chatting with AI as a metaphor for messaging a person, and Simrin noted how we already rely on metaphors like the trash can on a Mac, as long as they aren't too literal. When Andrew brought up the cloud icon for iCloud, Jun Hee went from finding it abstract to seeing it as brilliant, since a cloud is far away but always visible and can grow or shrink like stored data.

In week 8, Andrew and Juliette asked how the Norman door applies to AI. Crystal pointed out that AI agents let people ask questions naturally, but a simple interface doesn't make its abilities or limits clear. Jiho added that users can't always tell if an AI is giving facts, so a better design might mark information that has been fact checked. To me, this is just the Norman door in a new form.

Conclusion

Taken together, our class agrees that good design is about the person using it. Whether it was a door, a stove, a lock screen or an AI chat box, everyone came back to the idea that people shouldn't have to guess and that taking the users mental models into account while designing is good practice. The class sees UX and UI as connected, sees familiar metaphors as a way to help people learn, and sees simplicity as making things clear rather than empty. The AI discussion showed that simple is not always clear. In the end, we believe the best interfaces are the ones you barely notice because they just work.
