Micro Responses

15 responses chosen for the essay: all 9 after class reflections and 6 before class excerpts. They come from weeks 1, 2, 4, 5 and 8.

These responses cover how interfaces communicate what they can do and how that shapes whether people can use them with ease. They draw on Don Norman's "Norman Door" idea, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines, Google's Material Design, Rauno Freiberg's writing on the invisible details of interaction, and Olia Lialina's history of affordance.

Jasmine

Who was speaking
Juliette
What they said
Juliette mentioned that people may admire a physical design, such as a book, when it is unique and creative. However, in digital design, such as a website, users may feel frustrated when familiar elements like buttons or hamburger menus work differently from what they expect.
Their reflection
I found this interesting because my background is in graphic design. However, I realized this works differently in user interface design, where being too creative with familiar elements may actually frustrate users.

Matias
What they said
He mentioned a post he saw on LinkedIn that explained the relationship between UX and UI that said UX is the potato and UI is the mashed potato. You cannot make mashed potatoes without potatoes. Basically meaning that you cannot have UX without UI.
Their reflection
I thought the potato and mashed potato analogy was a good way to explain how UX and UI are connected, they both contribute to the same overall experience. You can't really separate them when designing a digital product, since the way something looks can affect how people use it, and the way people interact with it can influence how the visual design should work. They have to be considered together rather than treated as two completely separate parts.

After class · Week 5

Jun Hee Park

Who was speaking
Andrew
What they said
While discussing the metaphors used in UIUX design, Andrew brought up how the vault icon is used for the FileVault feature, which performs full disk encryption to protect your data from unauthorized access, and how the cloud icon is used for iCloud. The vault being used for FileVault is a great example of an ontological metaphor, as vaults were historically used to store something very special or important, since they are difficult to break into.
Their reflection
When I thought of metaphors used in interface design, iCloud was the very first thing that popped into my head. However, I didn't feel sure what the cloud image could possibly be referring to, as iCloud stores your data, and a cloud seemed abstract and irrelevant to what data storage actually does. While discussing this with the class, I came to the realization of how brilliant Apple's choice of the cloud was for iCloud. A cloud is far away yet you can always see it, always floating, can increase or decrease in size, and is one of the forms in the water cycle, all of which fit the description of what iCloud does.

After class · Week 8

Crystal Lam

Who was speaking
Andrew and Juliette
What they said
Asked how Norman Door's concept of affordances applies to AI agents and wearables. In response I focused on how the simple designs of AI agent interfaces and wearable afford natural interactions like asking questions or keeping it on and on body for daily use, and then the discussion moved toward whether a simple interface necessarily makes its functions clear to users and how a product can enable an action without clearly communicating its possibilities.
Their reflection
Connecting Norman Door to AI made me think about the distinction between an affordance, or what a technology allows users to do, and how clearly its design communicates those possibilities. I think conversational AI agents afford asking questions and delegating tasks through natural language, potentially making technology more accessible to people who struggle with traditional interfaces. However, users may not realize the full range of actions an AI agent can perform or understand its limitations, which made me consider how designers can make AI's affordances more discoverable and understandable rather than assuming that a simple interface makes its capabilities clear.

Before class · Week 4

Simrin Rajani

Agency. Stay out of the way. People use your product to get things done. Often the best way to help them do this is to get them directly to the task or content at hand. The best designs are unobtrusive and present when people need them. Give people the freedom to explore. Let them move through your interface and access features without being locked into specific flows or modes. When a guided flow is necessary, make it easy to skip or escape so people can get to the main experience quickly. Help people recover from mistakes. When people know they can reverse an action or return to a previous state, they feel free to explore, and that freedom makes your interface more inviting. Build forgiveness into your design, and make it easy. Recovering from the unexpected shouldn't cost people their time or work.
Source
Apple. "Design Principles." Human Interface Guidelines. developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/design-principles
